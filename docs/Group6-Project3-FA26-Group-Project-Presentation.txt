GetWork: An AI Framework to Match Job Applicants to Open Positions
AI Disclosure: These slides were generated by Claude (Anthropic), using our written proposal and the ASU brand guide presentation template as input.
We present GetWork, a two-way framework that matches resumes to job postings and postings to candidates, using a skill taxonomy generated from the corpus and an agent that explains each match.

Same skills, different vocabulary
Problem Definition
Job search and hiring are two sides of one matching problem: candidates sift through postings, recruiters screen applicant pools
Keyword screening (ATS) is transparent, but gives no credit to related skills and can screen out qualified applicants [7]
Embedding matching captures similarity beyond exact terms [14], but a single vector is hard to explain
Most recommenders search in one direction only, although the problem is reciprocal [4]

Research question
Does structuring resumes and postings around a corpus-generated skill taxonomy improve two-way matching over keyword and embedding baselines, while producing explanations that candidates and recruiters can act on?
A posting that requires React and a resume that lists Vue describe closely related work, yet share no keywords. Keyword systems miss this match, and embedding systems may find it but cannot say why. We target both directions of the search, and every match comes with an explanation.

+ Transparent and cheap

GetWork (ours)

+ Both directions from one matrix

Evaluation practice: LLM judges agree closely with human relevance labels [23], but favor their own outputs [17]; TREC pooling [24] and nDCG [9] are standard for ranked lists.
Keyword matching is what most applicant tracking systems use. Embedding models improve recall but lose explainability. Surveys of job recommenders note that reciprocal, two-way matching is important but rarely addressed. Our approach keeps the embedding space but makes related skills explicit through cluster labels.

NEW
highlights the updated evaluation plan based on proposal feedback
Development uses 10 resumes and 10 postings. The judges label them, and we review their rationales to fix the rubric before any test labeling. The test set is now balanced at 100 resumes and 100 postings, all labeled by the judges, and final metrics come from these labels. For 10 resumes and 10 postings from the test set, both authors also label the same pooled pairs, about 400 pairs. Authors label independently with no adjudication. We report author-author kappa and each judge's kappa against each author; the judge is treated as a third annotator and is considered reliable if its agreement with each author is close to author-author agreement. Human-label metrics are computed separately under each author's labels, and we report whether the system order holds under both. With only three systems, Kendall's tau can only take the values 1, 1/3, -1/3, or -1, so one swap between two nearly tied systems drops it to 1/3. We therefore also report the metric gaps between systems, for example Full minus B2 in nDCG@10, under judge and human labels.

Fallback rule for the human check. The rubric is frozen after the development stage, so the test pairs never shape it. Judges are compared against author-author agreement, not only the fixed 0.6 threshold. Here, human-judge kappa means the mean of the judges' kappa against each author. If human-judge kappa falls below 0.6 while author-author kappa is at least 0.6: (1) we split the human set in half, 5 resumes and 5 postings each; (2) we use the first half to diagnose the disagreements, checking each judge on its own, the lower-label-wins rule, the raw-text input, and the rubric; (3) we revise once and relabel the full test set; (4) we report human-judge kappa only on the untouched second half. If the judges are still clearly worse than the authors after one revision, the human-labeled queries become the primary results and the judge results are reported as secondary. If both kappa values are low, the task itself is subjective, and we report both side by side.
